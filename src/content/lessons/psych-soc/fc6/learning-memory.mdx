## Classical Conditioning: Learning by Association

**Classical conditioning** describes how an organism learns to associate two stimuli such that a previously neutral stimulus comes to trigger a response it never originally caused. Before conditioning, an **unconditioned stimulus (US)** naturally and automatically produces an **unconditioned response (UR)** — food (US) naturally causes salivation (UR), with no learning required. A **neutral stimulus (NS)**, such as a bell, produces no relevant response on its own. When the neutral stimulus is repeatedly paired with the unconditioned stimulus, it becomes a **conditioned stimulus (CS)** that, on its own, now elicits a **conditioned response (CR)** — the bell alone causes salivation.

A few process terms recur constantly on the exam. **Acquisition** is the initial learning of the CS-US association. **Extinction** occurs when the CS is repeatedly presented without the US, causing the CR to weaken and disappear. **Spontaneous recovery** is the reappearance of a weakened CR after a rest period, even without further pairing — evidence that extinction suppresses a response rather than erasing the underlying association. **Stimulus generalization** occurs when stimuli similar to the CS also evoke the CR; **stimulus discrimination** is the learned ability to distinguish the CS from similar-but-irrelevant stimuli and respond only to the true CS.

## Operant Conditioning: Learning from Consequences

Where classical conditioning links two stimuli, **operant conditioning** links a *behavior* to its *consequences* — organisms learn to repeat behaviors that produce favorable outcomes and suppress behaviors that produce unfavorable ones. The key distinction the exam tests relentlessly is that **reinforcement always increases** the future likelihood of a behavior, while **punishment always decreases** it — regardless of whether something is added or removed. Crossing "positive/negative" with "reinforcement/punishment" gives four categories:

| | Increases behavior | Decreases behavior |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Something added (positive) | Positive reinforcement (add a reward) | Positive punishment (add an aversive stimulus) |
| Something removed (negative) | Negative reinforcement (remove an aversive stimulus) | Negative punishment (remove a reward) |

The most commonly missed point here: **negative reinforcement is not punishment.** "Negative" refers only to removal, not to an unpleasant outcome — negative reinforcement (e.g., taking an aspirin removes a headache) still strengthens the behavior that caused the removal.

**Reinforcement schedules** describe the timing pattern of reinforcement, and they produce sharply different behavioral signatures. **Fixed-ratio** schedules reinforce after a set number of responses (a steady, high response rate with a brief post-reinforcement pause). **Variable-ratio** schedules reinforce after an unpredictable number of responses and produce the highest, steadiest response rate of any schedule while being the most resistant to extinction — this is the schedule underlying slot-machine gambling. **Fixed-interval** schedules reinforce the first response after a set amount of time, producing a characteristic scalloped pattern where responding increases as the interval's end approaches. **Variable-interval** schedules reinforce the first response after an unpredictable amount of time, producing a slow, steady response rate.

## Encoding, Storage, and Retrieval

Memory is conventionally modeled as three sequential stages. **Encoding** is the initial processing of information into a usable memory representation — deeper, more meaningful (semantic) encoding generally produces more durable memories than shallow, structural encoding. **Storage** is the retention of that encoded information over time, spanning sensory memory (a fraction of a second), short-term/working memory (seconds, limited to roughly 7 ± 2 items without rehearsal), and long-term memory (potentially permanent, with vast capacity). **Retrieval** is the process of accessing stored information, and it is where much of everyday "forgetting" actually occurs — information can be present in storage but temporarily inaccessible due to insufficient retrieval cues (as in tip-of-the-tongue states).

## The Serial Position Effect

A classic demonstration tying encoding and retrieval together is the **serial position effect**: when people are asked to recall a list of items, recall is best for items at the beginning of the list and at the end, and worst for items in the middle. The advantage for early items is the **primacy effect** — those items had time to be rehearsed and transferred into stable long-term memory before the list continued. The advantage for the final items is the **recency effect** — those items are still sitting in short-term/working memory at the moment of recall and are retrieved from there rather than from long-term storage. This distinction matters because the two effects respond differently to manipulation: a delay between list presentation and recall selectively eliminates the recency effect (short-term memory decays or gets displaced) while leaving the primacy effect intact, since primacy already reflects long-term consolidation.
