## From Stimulus to Signal: The Action Potential

Every sensation you experience — light, sound, pressure, taste — ultimately becomes the same currency inside the nervous system: an electrochemical signal called an **action potential**. A stimulus first triggers a sensory receptor to produce a graded receptor potential; if that depolarization is large enough to cross a fixed **threshold** at the axon hillock, the neuron fires a full action potential. Below threshold, nothing happens; once threshold is crossed, the neuron fires at full amplitude every time. This is the **all-or-nothing principle**, and it leads to one of the most heavily tested ideas in this content area: because every action potential looks identical in size, the nervous system cannot encode stimulus *intensity* by making individual spikes bigger. Instead, intensity is coded by **firing frequency** — a bright light or a loud sound causes a neuron to fire more action potentials per second, not larger ones.

Mechanistically, the action potential is driven by voltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ channels. Depolarization opens Na⁺ channels, Na⁺ rushes in and drives the membrane potential sharply positive; slightly delayed, voltage-gated K⁺ channels open and K⁺ exits, repolarizing (and briefly hyperpolarizing) the membrane. The Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase then restores the resting ionic gradients over time, but the immediate repolarization is a channel phenomenon, not a pump phenomenon.

## Saltatory Conduction and Myelin

Axons wrapped in **myelin** (produced by Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system and oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system) conduct signals dramatically faster than unmyelinated axons of the same diameter. Myelin is not continuous — it is interrupted at regular gaps called **nodes of Ranvier**, and voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are concentrated almost exclusively at these nodes. Because the myelinated segments are electrically insulated, the action potential does not regenerate continuously along the membrane; instead, it effectively jumps from node to node, a process called **saltatory conduction** ("saltatory" from the Latin for "leaping"). This is a speed mechanism only — myelin does not change the amplitude or all-or-nothing character of the action potential itself, it simply reduces how much membrane has to be actively depolarized per unit length of axon, cutting conduction time substantially.

## Detecting a Stimulus: Thresholds and Weber's Law

Psychophysics asks a different question than neurophysiology: not how a signal travels, but how much stimulus a person needs before they notice it at all. The **absolute threshold** is defined as the minimum intensity of a stimulus that can be detected correctly 50% of the time — not 100%, because detection is probabilistic and influenced by attention, fatigue, and noise, not a fixed on/off switch.

A second, equally testable threshold concept is the **difference threshold**, or **just noticeable difference (JND)** — the minimum change in a stimulus required for a person to detect that a change occurred at all. **Weber's law** describes how the JND scales with stimulus magnitude: the JND is a constant *proportion* of the original stimulus, not a constant absolute amount. Formally, ΔI/I = k, where I is the initial stimulus intensity, ΔI is the smallest detectable change, and k is a constant specific to that sensory dimension. This has a concrete, testable consequence: if you can barely detect the difference between a 100 g and 110 g weight (a 10% change), then detecting a similar difference at 1000 g will require a much larger absolute change — roughly 100 g, not 10 g — because it's the *ratio* of change to baseline that stays constant, not the raw increment.

## Sensation vs. Perception

Finally, this content area draws a sharp conceptual line between two terms that sound similar but are functionally distinct. **Sensation** is the raw detection of physical stimulus energy by sensory receptors and its conversion into neural signals (a process called **transduction**) — it is bottom-up and largely automatic. **Perception** is the brain's organization and interpretation of that sensory input into a meaningful experience — recognizing a pattern of light and dark as "a face," for instance. Perception is shaped heavily by **top-down processing**: prior knowledge, expectation, context, and motivation actively influence how raw sensory data is interpreted, sometimes even overriding it (as in many visual illusions, where the physical stimulus is constant but perceived experience is not). The exam frequently sets up scenarios where two people receive identical sensory input but perceive it differently — that gap is always a top-down, perceptual phenomenon, not a difference in sensation.

| Concept | What it describes | Key exam point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Absolute threshold | Minimum detectable stimulus | Detected 50% of the time, not always |
| Difference threshold (JND) | Minimum detectable *change* in stimulus | Governed by Weber's law |
| Sensation | Raw detection/transduction of stimulus energy | Bottom-up, largely automatic |
| Perception | Interpretation of sensory input into experience | Top-down, shaped by expectation/context |
