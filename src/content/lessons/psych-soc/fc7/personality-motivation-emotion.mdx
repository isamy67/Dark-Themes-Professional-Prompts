## The Trait Approach: The Big Five

Trait theories treat personality as a stable set of dimensions along which people vary continuously, rather than as fixed "types." The dominant trait model tested on the exam is the **Big Five** (also called the Five Factor Model, remembered by the acronym **OCEAN**): **Openness** to experience (curiosity, imagination, preference for novelty), **Conscientiousness** (organization, self-discipline, goal-directed behavior), **Extraversion** (sociability, assertiveness, positive affect in social settings), **Agreeableness** (cooperativeness, trust, compassion), and **Neuroticism** (emotional instability, tendency toward anxiety and negative affect). Each dimension is a continuum — a person is not simply "extraverted or not," they fall somewhere along a spectrum, and the five traits are considered relatively stable across adulthood and cross-culturally replicable, which is part of why this model has displaced older, more rigid typologies as the standard framework.

This is distinct from psychodynamic and humanistic approaches to personality, which the exam also references: psychodynamic theory (Freud) emphasizes unconscious conflict among id, ego, and superego, while humanistic theory (Rogers, Maslow) emphasizes conscious self-determination and growth toward one's full potential. The Big Five is agnostic about *why* traits exist and simply describes *how* people reliably differ.

## Motivation: Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

**Maslow's hierarchy of needs** organizes human motivation into an ordered pyramid, typically drawn with five (sometimes expanded to eight) levels: physiological needs (food, water, sleep) at the base, followed by safety needs, love/belonging needs, esteem needs, and **self-actualization** (realizing one's full personal potential) at the apex. The theory's core claim, and the part most often tested, is that lower-level needs generally must be *substantially* satisfied before higher-level needs become strong motivators of behavior — a person who is starving is unlikely to be motivated by esteem or creative fulfillment until physiological needs are addressed. This is a general tendency rather than an absolute law (real people sometimes pursue higher needs despite unmet lower ones), but the ordering itself — physiological → safety → belonging → esteem → self-actualization — is a well-defined, testable sequence.

## Theories of Emotion

Three classic theories compete to explain the relationship between physiological arousal, behavior, and the subjective feeling of emotion, and distinguishing them precisely is one of the highest-yield tasks in this section.

**James-Lange theory** proposes that physiological arousal comes *first* and the subjective emotion follows from — and is essentially our interpretation of — that bodily response: you see a bear, your heart races and you run, and it is the perception of that racing heart and running that *constitutes* the feeling of fear. Body precedes and causes emotion.

**Cannon-Bard theory** was developed partly as a critique of James-Lange, arguing that physiological arousal and the subjective experience of emotion occur **simultaneously and independently** of one another, both triggered directly by the same stimulus via the thalamus, without one causing the other. You see the bear, and your racing heart and your feeling of fear happen in parallel, not one causing the other.

**Schachter-Singer two-factor theory** adds a cognitive layer: emotion results from the combination of (1) physiological arousal and (2) a **cognitive label** applied to that arousal based on the surrounding context. Critically, this theory holds that arousal alone is relatively nonspecific — the same racing heart could be labeled "excitement," "fear," or "anger" depending on situational cues, and it is the cognitive appraisal that determines which emotion is actually experienced.

| Theory | Order of events | Key claim |
| --- | --- | --- |
| James-Lange | Stimulus → arousal → emotion | Emotion is the perception of bodily arousal |
| Cannon-Bard | Stimulus → (arousal and emotion simultaneously, independently) | Arousal does not cause emotion; both come from the stimulus in parallel |
| Schachter-Singer | Stimulus → arousal → cognitive labeling → emotion | Same arousal + different label = different emotion |

A useful way to keep these straight under time pressure: James-Lange is "body, then emotion"; Cannon-Bard is "body and emotion together, independently"; Schachter-Singer is "body plus thought about the body equals emotion."
