## Explaining Disorders: The Biopsychosocial and Diathesis-Stress Models

Modern clinical psychology and psychiatry reject single-cause explanations for mental disorders in favor of the **biopsychosocial model**, which holds that disorders arise from the interaction of biological factors (genetics, neurotransmitter function, brain structure), psychological factors (cognitive patterns, coping style, learned behaviors), and social factors (family environment, culture, socioeconomic stress, social support). The exam consistently rewards answer choices that acknowledge multiple interacting contributors over choices that attribute a disorder to one isolated cause.

A more specific, mechanistic version of this idea is the **diathesis-stress model**. A **diathesis** is a predisposition or vulnerability — often genetic or biological, though it can be a learned cognitive vulnerability — that on its own does not cause a disorder. A disorder emerges only when that underlying diathesis is activated by a sufficient **stressor**, an environmental trigger such as trauma, loss, or chronic strain. This model explains why two people with an identical genetic predisposition can have entirely different outcomes depending on their life circumstances, and why the same stressor can affect two people very differently depending on their underlying vulnerability. Diathesis-stress is essentially a specific application of the biopsychosocial model to the question of *when* a disorder actually manifests.

## Mood and Anxiety Disorders

**Mood disorders** are characterized by sustained, pervasive disturbances in emotional state. **Major depressive disorder** requires a period of at least two weeks featuring depressed mood or anhedonia (loss of interest/pleasure in previously enjoyable activities), plus additional symptoms (changes in sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, or feelings of worthlessness) severe enough to impair daily functioning. **Bipolar disorder** is defined by the presence of at least one manic (or, in bipolar II, hypomanic) episode — a period of abnormally elevated mood, energy, and impulsivity — which may alternate with depressive episodes; the presence or absence of mania, not simply "mood swings" in the colloquial sense, is what distinguishes bipolar disorder from unipolar depression.

**Anxiety disorders** involve excessive fear or worry that is disproportionate to actual threat and interferes with functioning. This category includes generalized anxiety disorder (chronic, diffuse worry not tied to a specific object), phobias (intense, focused fear of a specific object or situation), panic disorder (recurrent, unexpected panic attacks with intense physical symptoms), and social anxiety disorder. Although anxiety and depression are distinct diagnostic categories, they are highly comorbid and share overlapping biological substrates (notably dysregulation of serotonergic and noradrenergic signaling), which is why passages sometimes describe patients meeting features of both.

## Psychotic Disorders: Positive and Negative Symptoms

**Schizophrenia**, the prototypical psychotic disorder, is organized around a distinction the exam tests heavily: symptoms are classified as **positive** or **negative** based on whether they add something to normal experience or take something away, not based on whether they are "good" or "bad." **Positive symptoms** are abnormal experiences added on top of normal functioning — hallucinations (perceptions without an external stimulus, most commonly auditory), delusions (fixed false beliefs maintained despite contrary evidence), and disorganized speech or behavior. **Negative symptoms** represent a diminishment of normal function — flat or blunted affect, avolition (reduced motivation to initiate goal-directed behavior), alogia (reduced speech output), and social withdrawal. This distinction matters clinically as well as conceptually: antipsychotic medications that block dopamine receptors are generally far more effective against positive symptoms than negative ones, which has shaped both treatment approaches and biological theories of the disorder.

| Category | Examples | Core feature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mood disorders | Major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder | Sustained disturbance of emotional state |
| Anxiety disorders | GAD, phobias, panic disorder | Disproportionate fear/worry impairing function |
| Psychotic disorders | Schizophrenia | Positive symptoms (added) vs. negative symptoms (diminished) |

Across all three categories, diagnosis on the MCAT (and in the DSM framework it reflects) is never based on a single symptom in isolation — it requires a defined symptom cluster, a minimum duration, and evidence of functional impairment, which is why passage-based questions typically give you several converging details rather than one.
