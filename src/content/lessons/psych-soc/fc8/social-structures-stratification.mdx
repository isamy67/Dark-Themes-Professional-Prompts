## Three Lenses on Society

Sociology approaches "why society looks the way it does" through several competing theoretical perspectives, and the exam routinely presents a social phenomenon and asks which perspective best explains it. Recognizing each theory's core assumption is the fastest way to answer these correctly.

**Functionalism** (associated with Durkheim and Parsons) views society as a system of interdependent parts, each serving a function that contributes to overall social stability, much like organs in a body. From this lens, an institution or practice persists because it serves a genuine purpose — education persists because it socializes children and prepares a workforce; even phenomena that look dysfunctional (such as a moderate amount of crime) are analyzed for the stabilizing function they might indirectly serve (crime, in Durkheim's classic argument, clarifies and reinforces a society's moral boundaries). Functionalism tends to emphasize consensus and equilibrium.

**Conflict theory** (rooted in Marx) offers a sharply different lens: society is not a cooperative system but an arena of competition over scarce resources, and social structures persist not because they benefit everyone, but because they serve the interests of a dominant group at the expense of others. Where functionalism asks "what stabilizing purpose does this structure serve," conflict theory asks "whose interests does this structure serve, and who is disadvantaged by it." Applied to stratification specifically, conflict theorists argue that class hierarchies persist because those with power and resources actively work — through law, institutions, and ideology — to preserve their advantaged position.

**Symbolic interactionism** operates at a very different scale than the other two. Rather than analyzing large-scale institutions, it focuses on **micro-level**, everyday face-to-face interactions and the shared symbols and meanings people construct and negotiate through them. A symbolic interactionist studying "the family" would look not at family's function for society, but at how family members actually communicate, interpret roles, and construct shared meaning in their daily interactions. This macro/micro distinction — functionalism and conflict theory operate at the macro (societal/structural) level, symbolic interactionism at the micro (individual interaction) level — is itself a frequently tested point.

| Perspective | Core question | Level of analysis | View of stratification |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Functionalism | What function does this serve for society? | Macro | Stratification motivates people to fill necessary roles |
| Conflict theory | Whose interests does this serve? | Macro | Stratification preserves advantage for a dominant group |
| Symbolic interactionism | How do people construct shared meaning through interaction? | Micro | Stratification is enacted/reinforced in everyday interaction and labeling |

## Social Stratification

**Social stratification** refers to a society's division into hierarchical layers based on unequal access to valued resources — commonly analyzed along dimensions of **class** (economic position, often tied to income, wealth, and occupation), **status** (social prestige or honor), and **power** (capacity to influence others despite resistance). These dimensions often correlate but can diverge — a person can hold high status with modest income, or considerable power without broad social prestige.

Stratification systems vary in how much movement between layers is possible. A **caste system** is a closed stratification system in which social position is fixed at birth and essentially unchangeable, with little to no social mobility. A **class system**, by contrast, is comparatively open, allowing for **social mobility** — movement between strata based on factors like education, income change, or marriage. **Social mobility** is further divided into **vertical mobility** (movement up or down the hierarchy) and **horizontal mobility** (movement between positions of similar rank), and into **intragenerational mobility** (change within one person's lifetime) versus **intergenerational mobility** (change across generations, e.g., comparing a person's status to their parents').

Stratification is not simply an abstract sociological category — it structurally shapes access to education, healthcare, housing, and legal protection, which is precisely why the perspectives above disagree so sharply about whether stratification is a stabilizing necessity (functionalism) or an unjust structure maintained by those it benefits (conflict theory).
