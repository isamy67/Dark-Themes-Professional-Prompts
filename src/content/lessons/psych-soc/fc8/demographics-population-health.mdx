## Measuring Disease in a Population: Incidence vs. Prevalence

Population health depends on distinguishing two measures that sound similar but answer fundamentally different questions, and this distinction is tested constantly. **Incidence** is the number of *new* cases of a disease that arise in a defined population over a specified period of time — it measures the rate at which people are newly becoming affected. **Prevalence** is the total number of *existing* cases (new and old combined) in a population at a given point in time — it measures how much disease is currently present, regardless of when it started.

The relationship between the two depends heavily on disease duration and mortality, and this is where the exam likes to build tricky scenarios. A disease that is chronic and rarely fatal — well-controlled type 2 diabetes, for example — can have relatively low incidence (few brand-new cases each year) yet high prevalence, because affected individuals accumulate in the population over many years without dying or being cured. Conversely, a disease that is either rapidly fatal or quickly and completely cured (a severe acute infection, for instance) can have meaningful incidence but stay low in prevalence, because cases do not linger in the population — patients either die or recover quickly and drop out of the "currently affected" count. In general terms, prevalence is a function of both incidence and disease duration: prevalence ≈ incidence × average duration of disease.

## The Social Gradient in Health

A robust and repeatedly replicated finding in population health is the **social gradient in health**: health outcomes improve in a stepwise fashion across the entire range of socioeconomic status (SES), not merely at the boundary between poverty and non-poverty. This is a critical nuance — the gradient is not a simple threshold effect where only the poorest group has worse health while everyone above some income line is equivalent. Instead, each incremental step up the SES ladder — in income, education, or occupational status — is associated with measurably better average health outcomes, even comparing the moderately well-off to the very wealthy. This pattern has been observed across an enormous range of outcomes, from cardiovascular disease to life expectancy, and across many different societies, which is part of why it's treated as a robust, generalizable phenomenon rather than an artifact of any one country's healthcare system.

## Explaining the SES-Health Correlation: Causation vs. Selection

Observing that low SES correlates with worse health does not, on its own, establish *why* — and the exam frequently asks you to reason about which mechanism a given piece of evidence supports. Two major, non-mutually-exclusive explanations compete:

**Social causation** holds that low socioeconomic status directly produces worse health, through pathways such as chronic psychosocial stress, cumulative physiological wear (allostatic load), reduced access to healthcare and healthy food, more hazardous living and working environments, and less control over one's daily circumstances. In this model, the arrow of causation runs from social position to health.

**Social selection** (sometimes called **health selection** or **social drift**) proposes the reverse causal direction: poor health — whether from chronic illness, disability, or mental illness — limits a person's earning capacity, educational attainment, and occupational opportunities over time, causing them to drift downward in socioeconomic status (or preventing upward mobility in the first place). In this model, health problems come first and shape social position, rather than the other way around.

These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and most researchers believe both mechanisms operate simultaneously and can even reinforce each other in a feedback loop. The key skill the exam tests is recognizing that a simple cross-sectional correlation between SES and health **cannot, by itself, distinguish between these two directions of causation** — doing so requires longitudinal data that tracks whether SES changes precede health changes, or vice versa, within the same individuals over time.

| Explanation | Direction of causation | Example mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Social causation | Low SES → worse health | Chronic stress, reduced healthcare access, allostatic load |
| Social selection / drift | Worse health → low SES | Illness limits earning capacity and mobility |
