## Strengthen and Weaken: Target the Actual Conclusion

Strengthen and weaken questions present new, hypothetical information and ask how it affects the author's argument. The most important discipline here is identifying the author's **actual, specific conclusion** before evaluating any answer choice — because the most common wrong answers are built to affect a nearby but slightly different claim, a straw-man version of what the author really argued.

Take this excerpt:

> Advocates argue that requiring standardized certification for independent translators would improve the accuracy of legal document translation. But certification exams test general-purpose language proficiency, not the specialized vocabulary of contract law, immigration statutes, or medical consent forms — the very texts where translation errors carry the highest stakes. A credential aimed at general fluency is unlikely to move the needle on the specific problem it is invoked to solve.

The author's actual conclusion is narrow: general-fluency certification is unlikely to fix *specialized, high-stakes* translation errors. It is not a conclusion that certification is worthless, or that translators shouldn't be tested at all. A weaken answer like "certification exams have been shown to improve general conversational translation accuracy" doesn't touch the actual conclusion — it's true of a claim the author never made. A genuine weaken answer would need to attack the specific link, such as "certified translators make significantly fewer errors on legal and medical documents specifically, despite the exam's general-fluency focus." A genuine strengthen answer would support that same specific link, such as "adding a specialized legal-vocabulary module to the certification exam produced no measurable improvement in contract-translation accuracy" — evidence that the general/specialized gap the author describes is real. Notice that both examples engage the *precise* claim about specialized accuracy, not the general topic of translation quality.

## Isolate the Premise-to-Conclusion Link

Arguments move from premises to a conclusion, and the strongest weaken answers attack the *connection* between them rather than simply contradicting the conclusion outright. In the excerpt above, the premise is "certification tests general fluency, not specialized vocabulary"; the conclusion is "certification won't fix specialized translation errors." A weaken answer that attacks the *link* — for instance, showing that general fluency and specialized-document accuracy are, in practice, highly correlated — is more precise and more powerful than an answer that just asserts "translation quality is fine as it is," which doesn't engage with the author's actual reasoning at all.

## Apply Questions: Match the Principle, Not the Topic

**Apply** questions describe a new scenario, often from an unrelated field, and ask which one best fits the passage's underlying logic. The trap is picking the scenario that shares surface-level *subject matter* with the passage rather than the one that shares its *principle*. The translation passage above isn't really about translation — its underlying principle is something like "a general-purpose credential does not guarantee competence in a specialized, high-stakes subset of that same skill." A scenario about a general medical license not guaranteeing competence in a rare surgical subspecialty matches that principle exactly, even though it has nothing to do with language. A scenario about a translation certification program raising overall enrollment numbers, by contrast, stays on-topic but ignores the principle entirely, and would be a trap for anyone matching by keyword rather than by logic.

## The Common Trap: True but Irrelevant

Across both strengthen/weaken and apply questions, the most reliable trap answer is one that is factually plausible, or even clearly true, but simply does not bear on the argument's logic. "Legal-translation errors have decreased over the past decade" might be true and might even relate to the passage's topic, but if it doesn't specifically speak to whether certification would or wouldn't address specialized-vocabulary errors, it cannot strengthen or weaken the argument. Before selecting an answer, ask not "is this true?" but "if this were true, would it actually change how confident I am in the author's specific conclusion?" Relevance to the argument's logic, not truth alone, is what these questions are grading.
