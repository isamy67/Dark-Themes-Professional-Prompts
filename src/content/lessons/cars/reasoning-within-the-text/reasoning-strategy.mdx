## Main Point Questions: Cover the Whole Arc

A **main point** question asks you to state what the entire passage is doing, not what any single paragraph says. The most common wrong answer choices on these questions are true statements pulled from just one paragraph — accurate in isolation, but too narrow to represent the passage as a whole. The correct answer has to be broad enough to encompass the introduction, the body, and the conclusion, while still being specific enough that it couldn't describe a different passage entirely.

A useful test: for each candidate answer, ask "would this sentence make sense as something the author says in the *last* paragraph, summarizing everything before it?" If an answer choice only reflects paragraph two, it fails this test even if it's factually correct. Take a passage that opens by praising a city-planning theory, spends two paragraphs raising objections to it, and closes by arguing the theory still has practical value despite its flaws. The main point is not "the theory has flaws" (too narrow — that's the middle) and not "the theory is good" (ignores the objections) — it's something like "despite legitimate objections, the theory retains practical value," because that's the only option that reflects the passage's full shape.

## Function Questions: Check What Surrounds the Detail

A **function** question asks *why* the author included a specific sentence, example, or phrase — not what it means literally, but what job it's doing in the argument. The fastest way to answer these correctly is to read the sentence immediately before and immediately after the detail in question, because a detail's function is defined by its relationship to its neighbors, not by its content alone.

Consider this excerpt:

> Proponents of the policy point to a 12 percent drop in emergency-room visits in the pilot region. Yet emergency-room visits in neighboring, unaffected regions fell by a comparable 9 percent over the same period — a decline attributable to an unrelated statewide public-health campaign. The pilot's headline statistic, in other words, cannot be credited to the policy itself.

If a question asked why the author mentions the 9 percent decline in neighboring regions, the temptation is to answer "to provide a statistic for comparison" — true, but too generic. Reading the sentence that follows ("cannot be credited to the policy itself") reveals the actual function: this detail is a **counter-evidence device**, used to undercut the proponents' causal claim. The function is argumentative, not merely descriptive — it's there to weaken a specific claim made one sentence earlier.

## Inference Questions: Must Be True, Not Just Plausible

**Inference** questions are frequently missed because test-takers accept an answer that sounds *reasonable* rather than one that is *logically guaranteed* by the passage. An inference is a claim that must follow from what is stated — if you can construct any realistic scenario in which the passage is true but the answer choice is false, that choice is wrong, no matter how sensible it seems.

Using the excerpt above, "the policy had no positive effect on emergency-room visits" is too strong to infer — the passage shows the headline statistic is not clearly attributable to the policy, not that the policy definitely did nothing. A safer inference: "the 12 percent figure alone is insufficient to establish that the policy caused the drop in visits." That claim must be true given what the author says; the stronger causal claim is not.

## Reading Tone from Word Choice and Hedging

Authors rarely announce their attitude directly ("I am skeptical of this view"). Instead, tone is carried by word choice and hedging language. Phrases like "merely," "so-called," or "purportedly" signal skepticism; words like "compelling," "rigorous," or "illuminating" signal approval; heavy qualifiers ("may," "in some cases," "it is possible that") signal caution rather than conviction. In the excerpt above, "cannot be credited" is a firm, confident phrase — this author is not hedging, and a question asking about their tone toward the policy's proponents should lean skeptical or critical, not neutral. Training yourself to notice this vocabulary as you read is what lets you answer tone and attitude questions without having to re-scan the whole passage from scratch.
