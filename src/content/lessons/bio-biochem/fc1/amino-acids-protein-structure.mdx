## Amino Acids: Building Blocks with Chemical Personality

Every amino acid shares the same core skeleton — a central alpha carbon bonded to an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen, and a variable **side chain (R group)**. It's that side chain, and only that side chain, that gives each of the 20 standard amino acids its distinct chemical personality and determines how it behaves inside a folded protein.

Side chains fall into four broad functional categories, and sorting an amino acid into the correct category is one of the most frequently tested skills on the exam:

- **Nonpolar (hydrophobic)** — side chains dominated by carbon and hydrogen, such as alanine, valine, leucine, and phenylalanine. These avoid water and tend to bury themselves in the interior of a folded protein or within a lipid membrane.
- **Polar (uncharged)** — side chains with electronegative atoms (oxygen, nitrogen, or sulfur in the case of cysteine) capable of hydrogen bonding but without a net charge at physiological pH, such as serine, threonine, and asparagine.
- **Acidic (negatively charged)** — aspartate and glutamate, which carry a deprotonated carboxylate side chain at physiological pH and are negatively charged.
- **Basic (positively charged)** — lysine, arginine, and histidine, which carry a protonated, positively charged side chain at or near physiological pH (histidine's side chain has a pKa close to physiological pH, so its charge is the most context-dependent of the three).

This classification matters because it predicts where an amino acid ends up in three-dimensional structure: nonpolar residues cluster in a protein's hydrophobic core or in transmembrane segments, while polar and charged residues tend to sit on the solvent-exposed surface, interacting with water or with other charged residues to form salt bridges.

## The Four Levels of Protein Structure

Proteins are organized hierarchically, and each level of structure is built from — and depends on — the level below it.

**Primary structure** is simply the linear sequence of amino acids joined by peptide bonds, read from the N-terminus to the C-terminus. It is specified entirely by the genetic code and contains all the information needed, in principle, to determine everything above it. Because primary structure is the foundation, a single amino acid substitution can ripple upward and change a protein's folding, stability, or function — this is the logic behind many single-point genetic diseases.

**Secondary structure** refers to local, repeating folding patterns stabilized by hydrogen bonds between backbone atoms (the carbonyl oxygen and amide hydrogen of the peptide bond itself, not the side chains). The two dominant patterns are the **alpha helix**, a right-handed coil held together by hydrogen bonds between residues spaced four apart, and the **beta sheet**, formed by hydrogen bonding between adjacent, extended strands that run either parallel or antiparallel to one another.

**Tertiary structure** is the overall three-dimensional shape of a single folded polypeptide chain, produced as secondary-structure elements pack together and side chains find energetically favorable arrangements. Tertiary structure is stabilized by a wider range of interactions than secondary structure: hydrophobic packing in the core, hydrogen bonds and ionic (salt bridge) interactions between polar or charged side chains, and — for cysteine residues in particular — **disulfide bonds**, covalent bonds formed by the oxidation of two cysteine thiol groups. Disulfide bonds are distinctive because they are covalent rather than the weak, noncovalent interactions that stabilize most of secondary and tertiary structure, making them considerably more resistant to disruption.

**Quaternary structure** exists only in proteins built from more than one polypeptide chain (subunit), and describes how those separate chains associate into a functional complex. Hemoglobin, with its four subunits, is the textbook example: each subunit has its own primary through tertiary structure, and the functional protein exists only when all four associate together.

| Structure level | What it describes | Key stabilizing interactions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | Linear amino acid sequence | Covalent peptide bonds |
| Secondary | Local folding (helices, sheets) | Backbone hydrogen bonds |
| Tertiary | 3D fold of one chain | Hydrophobic packing, ionic bonds, disulfide bonds |
| Quaternary | Assembly of multiple chains | Same noncovalent/covalent interactions, between subunits |

## Denaturation: Losing Structure Without Losing Sequence

**Denaturation** is the disruption of a protein's secondary, tertiary, and (if present) quaternary structure, typically caused by heat, extreme pH, or chemical denaturants that disrupt hydrogen bonds and other weak interactions. A denatured protein unfolds into a disordered, usually nonfunctional conformation.

This is a frequently tested distinction: denaturation does **not** break the peptide bonds of the primary structure. The amino acid sequence remains completely intact — what's lost is everything built on top of it. This is precisely why some denatured proteins can, under the right conditions, spontaneously **renature** and refold back into their functional shape once the denaturing condition is removed: the primary sequence still contains all the information needed to specify the correct fold, a principle famously demonstrated by Anfinsen's ribonuclease refolding experiments.
