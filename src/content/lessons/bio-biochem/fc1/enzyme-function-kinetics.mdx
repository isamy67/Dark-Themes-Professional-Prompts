## Why Enzymes Matter

Nearly every reaction that keeps a cell alive is, on its own, far too slow to sustain life. Enzymes solve this by acting as biological catalysts: they speed up reactions by **lowering the activation energy** (Ea) — the energy barrier a reaction must climb before it can proceed — without being consumed themselves and without changing the reaction's overall thermodynamics.

That last point is worth stating precisely, because it is one of the most commonly tested distinctions on the exam: an enzyme changes *how fast* a reaction reaches equilibrium, not *whether* it reaches equilibrium, and not the free-energy difference (ΔG) between reactants and products. A reaction that is thermodynamically unfavorable stays unfavorable with or without an enzyme present.

## The Michaelis-Menten Model

Most enzymes follow a predictable kinetic pattern described by the Michaelis-Menten equation. Two values summarize an enzyme's behavior:

- **Vmax** — the maximum reaction velocity, reached once the enzyme is fully saturated with substrate.
- **Km** — the substrate concentration at which reaction velocity is half of Vmax.

Km is often described as an inverse measure of affinity: a **lower** Km means the enzyme reaches half-maximal velocity at a lower substrate concentration, which means it binds substrate more tightly (higher apparent affinity). A **higher** Km means the opposite — the enzyme needs more substrate around before it works efficiently.

At low substrate concentrations, velocity rises roughly linearly with substrate. As substrate concentration increases, the curve bends and eventually plateaus at Vmax, once essentially every enzyme molecule is occupied.

## Four Patterns of Enzyme Inhibition

Inhibitors reduce enzyme activity, and the exam consistently tests whether you can read a Vmax/Km pattern and correctly identify *which kind* of inhibition produced it.

| Inhibitor type | Effect on Vmax | Effect on Km | Overcome by excess substrate? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Competitive | Unchanged | Increases | Yes |
| Noncompetitive | Decreases | Unchanged | No |
| Uncompetitive | Decreases | Decreases | No |
| Mixed | Decreases | Increases or decreases | No |

A **competitive inhibitor** physically competes with substrate for the active site — usually because it resembles the substrate structurally. Because it's a competition, adding enough substrate can out-compete the inhibitor and still reach the same Vmax, just at a higher apparent Km.

A **noncompetitive inhibitor** binds a site other than the active site (an allosteric site) and distorts the enzyme's shape enough to impair catalysis regardless of how much substrate is present. Since it doesn't block substrate binding directly, Km is unaffected — but Vmax drops, because some fraction of enzyme is always disabled no matter the substrate concentration.

## Enzymes Are Sensitive to Their Environment

Because enzyme activity depends on maintaining a precise three-dimensional shape, most enzymes have a narrow optimal pH and temperature range. Outside that range, weak bonds stabilizing the enzyme's structure (hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions) break down, the enzyme **denatures**, and activity falls — often irreversibly at extremes. This is why, for example, most human enzymes work best near physiological pH and body temperature, and why a high fever or severe acidosis can meaningfully impair enzyme-dependent processes.
