## Oxygen Delivery and the Hemoglobin Dissociation Curve

Hemoglobin's ability to pick up oxygen in the lungs and release it in metabolically active tissue is best understood through the **oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve**, which plots hemoglobin oxygen saturation against the partial pressure of oxygen. The curve's sigmoidal shape reflects **cooperative binding** — binding of the first oxygen molecule increases the affinity of hemoglobin's remaining subunits for additional oxygen.

The curve's *position* is what the exam tests most heavily. A **rightward shift** represents decreased hemoglobin affinity for oxygen — hemoglobin releases oxygen more readily at any given partial pressure of oxygen — and is produced by increased CO2, increased H+ (decreased pH), increased temperature, and increased 2,3-BPG. The CO2/H+-driven component of this rightward shift is specifically called the **Bohr effect**. This is physiologically elegant: actively metabolizing tissue produces more CO2 and lactic acid, raising local CO2 and H+ and lowering local pH, which shifts the curve right and causes hemoglobin to unload more oxygen exactly where it's needed most. A **leftward shift** represents increased affinity — hemoglobin holds onto oxygen more tightly — and occurs under the opposite conditions (decreased CO2, decreased H+, decreased temperature), as well as with fetal hemoglobin, which has intrinsically higher oxygen affinity than adult hemoglobin to pull oxygen across the placenta.

## The Nephron: Filtration, Reabsorption, Secretion

The functional unit of the kidney, the **nephron**, processes blood plasma through three sequential steps. **Glomerular filtration** occurs at the glomerulus, where blood pressure forces water and small solutes out of capillaries and into Bowman's capsule, while cells and large proteins are normally retained in the bloodstream. **Tubular reabsorption** then recovers most of the useful filtered water, ions, and nutrients (like glucose and amino acids) back into the peritubular capillaries as the filtrate flows through the proximal tubule, loop of Henle, and distal tubule. **Tubular secretion** moves additional waste products and excess ions directly from peritubular blood into the tubule, supplementing what filtration alone removed. What remains after all three steps is excreted as urine.

The **loop of Henle** deserves special attention because it does something no other nephron segment does: it establishes the concentration gradient in the renal medulla that makes concentrated urine possible at all. The descending limb is permeable to water but not solutes, so water leaves and the filtrate becomes progressively more concentrated as it descends. The thick ascending limb is impermeable to water but actively pumps out ions (via the Na+/K+/2Cl- cotransporter), diluting the filtrate as it ascends while depositing solute into the surrounding medullary interstitium. This countercurrent arrangement means fluid leaving the loop is actually dilute, but the surrounding medullary tissue has been left highly concentrated — that standing gradient is what allows the collecting duct, downstream, to reabsorb water and concentrate urine when needed.

## Hormonal Fine-Tuning: ADH and Aldosterone

Two hormones make the final adjustments to fluid and electrolyte balance at the distal nephron and collecting duct, and distinguishing their targets is a frequently tested point.

**Antidiuretic hormone (ADH, also called vasopressin)** is released from the posterior pituitary in response to increased plasma osmolality (or decreased blood volume) and acts on the **collecting duct**, inserting aquaporin water channels into the luminal membrane and increasing water reabsorption. More ADH means more water is reclaimed and urine becomes more concentrated; less ADH means more water is excreted and urine becomes dilute.

**Aldosterone**, released from the adrenal cortex as part of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system in response to low blood pressure or low blood Na+, acts primarily on the **distal tubule and collecting duct** to increase Na+ reabsorption (water follows osmotically), while simultaneously increasing K+ and H+ secretion into the tubule. The key distinction: ADH regulates water reabsorption directly and independent of sodium, while aldosterone regulates sodium reabsorption directly, with water following passively as a secondary consequence.

| Hormone | Release trigger | Primary site of action | Primary effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ADH | ↑ plasma osmolality, ↓ blood volume | Collecting duct | ↑ water reabsorption |
| Aldosterone | ↓ blood pressure / ↓ Na+ (via RAAS) | Distal tubule, collecting duct | ↑ Na+ reabsorption, ↑ K+/H+ secretion |

## Baroreceptor Reflex: Short-Term Blood Pressure Control

While ADH and aldosterone provide longer-term regulation of blood volume, moment-to-moment blood pressure is controlled by the **baroreceptor reflex**. Stretch-sensitive baroreceptors located in the carotid sinus and aortic arch continuously monitor arterial wall stretch as a proxy for blood pressure. A drop in blood pressure reduces baroreceptor firing, which the medulla interprets as a signal to increase sympathetic outflow and decrease parasympathetic outflow — increasing heart rate, increasing cardiac contractility, and constricting blood vessels to raise blood pressure back toward its set point. A rise in blood pressure produces the opposite adjustment. This reflex operates on a timescale of seconds, making it the body's fastest blood pressure correction mechanism, complementing the slower, hormone-driven renal mechanisms that manage blood volume over hours to days.
