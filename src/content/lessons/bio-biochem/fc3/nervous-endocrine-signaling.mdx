## Two Systems, Two Speeds

The body coordinates its organ systems using two complementary communication networks that trade off speed for reach. The **nervous system** signals through electrical impulses along neurons and rapid, point-to-point chemical release at synapses, producing effects on the order of milliseconds that are tightly localized to the cells receiving the signal. The **endocrine system** signals through hormones released into the bloodstream, producing effects that take minutes to hours to develop but that reach essentially every tissue the circulation touches and can last far longer.

This speed/reach tradeoff is one of the most conceptually testable ideas in this material: a reflex withdrawal from a hot stove needs the millisecond precision of nervous signaling, while regulating basal metabolic rate over hours to days is far better suited to the sustained, broadcast nature of endocrine signaling. Many physiological responses — like the stress response — actually use both systems together, with fast neural signaling triggering an initial reaction that a slower hormonal cascade then sustains.

## How Hormones Reach Their Targets: Lipophilic vs. Hydrophilic

Hormones fall into two broad classes based on their chemistry, and that chemistry dictates where their receptors are located and how quickly their effects unfold.

**Steroid hormones** (such as cortisol, estrogen, and testosterone) and thyroid hormone are lipophilic, so they diffuse directly across the lipid bilayer of the plasma membrane. Their receptors are therefore located intracellularly — in the cytoplasm or nucleus — and the hormone-receptor complex typically acts directly as a transcription factor, binding DNA and altering gene expression. Because this route runs through transcription and new protein synthesis, steroid hormone effects tend to have a slower onset but longer duration.

**Peptide and amine hormones** (such as insulin, glucagon, and epinephrine) are generally hydrophilic and cannot cross the lipid bilayer, so their receptors sit on the cell surface. Binding triggers an intracellular second-messenger cascade — such as cAMP or the IP3/DAG pathway — that rapidly amplifies the signal and alters the activity of existing proteins, producing effects with much faster onset than steroid hormones, though typically shorter duration since no new gene transcription is required.

## Endocrine Axes and Negative Feedback

Most endocrine regulation is organized into hierarchical axes running from the hypothalamus to the pituitary to a peripheral endocrine gland, and the dominant regulatory logic throughout these axes is **negative feedback**: rising levels of the downstream hormone suppress the upstream signals that stimulated its release.

Take the **hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis** as the template. The hypothalamus releases thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH), which stimulates the anterior pituitary to release thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), which stimulates the thyroid gland to release thyroid hormone (T3/T4). Rising T3/T4 then feeds back to suppress both TRH and TSH release, keeping thyroid hormone levels within a tight physiological range. The **hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis** follows the identical top-down logic with corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), and cortisol.

Because feedback in these axes is negative rather than positive, understanding the direction of a hormonal abnormality lets you localize where in the axis a problem originates — a downstream gland failing to produce enough hormone causes upstream signals to rise as the feedback brake is released, while an upstream signaling failure causes both the upstream and downstream hormones to fall together.

## The Autonomic Nervous System: Sympathetic vs. Parasympathetic

The autonomic nervous system, which governs involuntary functions, is divided into two branches that generally act as **physiological antagonists** on shared target organs. The **sympathetic** division mobilizes the body for action — the "fight or flight" response — increasing heart rate and blood pressure, diverting blood flow toward skeletal muscle and away from digestion, and dilating the pupils and bronchioles. The **parasympathetic** division promotes "rest and digest" activity — slowing heart rate, stimulating digestive secretions and motility, and constricting the pupils.

Most organs that receive input from both branches show this antagonism directly, which is why the exam frequently tests predicting an organ's response given only one branch's activation: knowing the general sympathetic/parasympathetic pattern lets you infer the opposite branch's effect even for an organ or receptor you haven't memorized directly.
