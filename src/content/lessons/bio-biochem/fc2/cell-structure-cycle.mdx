## Organelles: Matching Structure to Function

Eukaryotic cells compartmentalize their work into membrane-bound organelles, and the exam consistently tests whether you can match an organelle to its primary function rather than just recognize its name.

The **mitochondrion** is the site of oxidative phosphorylation and the majority of ATP production. Its double membrane is functionally important: the inner membrane, folded into cristae to maximize surface area, houses the electron transport chain and ATP synthase, while the intermembrane space is where the proton gradient that drives ATP synthase accumulates.

The **rough endoplasmic reticulum (rough ER)**, studded with ribosomes, is the site where proteins destined for secretion, insertion into membranes, or delivery to organelles of the endomembrane system are synthesized and begin folding. The **smooth ER**, lacking ribosomes, instead handles lipid synthesis, steroid hormone synthesis, and detoxification of drugs and other compounds.

The **Golgi apparatus** receives proteins and lipids from the ER and further modifies them — including glycosylation — before sorting and packaging them into vesicles addressed to their final destination, whether that's the plasma membrane, a lysosome, or secretion outside the cell.

Other high-yield organelles include the **lysosome**, which contains acid hydrolases for breaking down macromolecules and worn-out organelles, and the **peroxisome**, which breaks down fatty acids via beta-oxidation and detoxifies reactive oxygen species.

## Membrane Transport: Does It Cost ATP?

Moving substances across the plasma membrane falls into two broad categories, and distinguishing them is a frequent point of confusion.

**Passive transport** moves a substance down its concentration (or electrochemical) gradient and requires no direct energy input. This includes **simple diffusion** of small nonpolar or uncharged molecules directly through the lipid bilayer, **facilitated diffusion** of polar or charged molecules through channel or carrier proteins, and **osmosis**, the diffusion of water itself across a semipermeable membrane toward the side with higher solute concentration.

**Active transport** moves a substance against its concentration gradient and therefore requires energy. **Primary active transport** uses ATP hydrolysis directly, as in the Na+/K+-ATPase, which pumps 3 Na+ out of the cell and 2 K+ into the cell per ATP hydrolyzed, maintaining the ion gradients essential for resting membrane potential and secondary transport. **Secondary active transport** doesn't use ATP directly but instead harnesses the electrochemical gradient of one ion (often Na+, itself maintained by a primary active pump) to drive the movement of a second solute against its own gradient, either in the same direction (symport) or the opposite direction (antiport).

## The Cell Cycle and Its Checkpoints

The eukaryotic cell cycle proceeds through **G1** (cell growth, organelle duplication), **S phase** (DNA replication), **G2** (further growth and preparation for division), and **M phase** (mitosis and cytokinesis). G1, S, and G2 are collectively called interphase.

Progression through the cycle is not automatic — it is enforced by **checkpoints** that verify each stage has completed correctly before allowing the cell to proceed to the next:

- The **G1/S checkpoint** (often called the restriction point) verifies the cell has adequate size, nutrients, and undamaged DNA before committing to replication.
- The **G2/M checkpoint** verifies DNA replication completed accurately and checks for DNA damage before allowing the cell to enter mitosis.
- The **spindle assembly checkpoint** (metaphase checkpoint), during M phase itself, verifies that every chromosome is properly attached to spindle fibers from both poles before allowing anaphase to proceed, preventing unequal chromosome segregation.

**p53** is the central tumor-suppressor protein enforcing the G1/S checkpoint. When DNA damage is detected, p53 halts the cycle to allow time for repair; if damage is too extensive to repair, p53 can instead trigger apoptosis (programmed cell death), eliminating the damaged cell entirely rather than letting it divide.

## When Checkpoints Fail

Checkpoints exist specifically to prevent a cell with damaged or incompletely replicated DNA from dividing and passing that damage on to daughter cells. When checkpoint proteins like p53 are lost or mutated, damaged cells that should have been halted or eliminated continue cycling instead, accumulating further mutations with each division. This loss of checkpoint control — allowing uncontrolled, unchecked proliferation — is considered a defining hallmark of cancer, and it's the conceptual link the exam most often draws between basic cell cycle biology and disease.
